Four things my resume only touched on. Learn more on what I built, how it holds together, and where I am still required.

01

How I built an analysis pipeline that renders its own board

Qualitative analysis is trusted only when a conclusion can be followed back to what a participant actually said. I built the pipeline so that path survives every step.

Agents work in parallel, one transcript each, none of them able to see another participant's material; one person's interpretation cannot quietly shape the next. Each proposes candidate findings tied to the exact rows of transcript behind them. Separate passes then challenge that work: one filters material that is context or topic rather than interior reasoning, another tests for over-compression and for interpretation that has drifted past its evidence.

Around all of it sits ordinary software, not AI. It decides what runs in what order, what each agent may read or write, and what gets recorded. Findings resolve into a small number of cards per participant, and nothing advances to a finding without a person accepting it.

The board is generated from that run rather than maintained by hand. Cards, groupings, evidence links, and the tests applied to each grouping are rendered onto a shared Miro surface directly from versioned output, so what analysts look at can be reproduced from source: a visual change always has a run behind it. Analysts get a working surface without anyone becoming its caretaker.

Agent-assisted, not agentic
The infrastructure around the analysis may be agentic. The analysis itself stays agent-assisted: agents propose, challenge, and compare; a researcher remains accountable for what becomes a finding.

02

Why bias, generalization, and compression break a trace

Three things destroy the path back to the evidence, and they rarely announce themselves. My own bias arrives first: I recognize a pattern because I have seen it before, not because this participant put it there. Generalization follows, widening a label until it covers people who reasoned their way to the same behavior for unrelated reasons. Compression finishes the job, folding several ways of thinking into one confident phrase that reads better than any of them.

None of the three produces an obviously wrong answer. That is what makes them expensive. A clean result is indistinguishable from a lucky one, so I make the system force the question. One test I use routinely: rewrite every card as only what the person did, then see whether the group falls apart. If it does, the shared behavior was never the thing holding it together, and the label was mine rather than theirs.

So a grouping becomes defensible only when a reader can see what it beat: which alternative arrangement was tried, which test it failed, and what replaced it. I keep the discarded groupings as negative cases, in the run and on the board. A theme that sounded coherent and did not survive contact with the evidence is the clearest proof I have that the compression was caught rather than shipped.

Trace
The recorded path from a finished claim back to the original evidence: which transcript, which rows, which agent proposed it, under which version of the instructions, and who accepted it.

03

What a year of method practice taught me

For more than a year I analyzed interview transcripts alongside Indi Young and a small team of practicing researchers, contributing to the Finding Thinking Styles practice as its training program was established and joining its first sessions.

The method runs from extraction of interior cognition through provisional grouping, coherence and distinctness testing, and the rejection of groupings the evidence will not carry. The work is weekly and collaborative. Each analyst is responsible for particular people, and a card on the board is not a category candidate; it stands for someone whose reasoning that analyst agreed to represent. Placing it requires explaining why it belongs, out loud, to others who will push back. Sometimes an analyst speaks provisionally in the first person, from the participant's point of view, which changes what the group is willing to accept: the card stops being a label and becomes a logic someone has to answer for.

What that practice taught me is not a technique but a set of refusals. Topics are convenient. Demographics are convenient. A polished phrase is convenient. The first grouping that looks coherent is the most convenient thing of all. The method is built to resist each of them, and it does so by making every analyst answerable rather than neutral.

This is the discipline my systems work is trying to be worthy of. It is also why those systems stop where they do.

Attribution
The method is Indi Young's, developed over decades and taught by her. She does not use AI agents to produce thinking styles; the agent work described here is my own extension, independent and unofficial, and not affiliated with or endorsed by her.

04

Where I keep the evidence after a study ends

A finding that cannot be found again is a finding an organization pays for twice. Three pieces of my work address that directly.

The Qualitative Analysis Agent Manifest is the configuration the agents actually run under: 62 rules covering quality standards, process aids, tactical and judgment checks, and the failure checks that reject weak output. It states what the method seeks, which distinctions must be preserved, how a claim stays linked to source language, and where human review is required. Writing the method down this way is what makes it auditable: a reviewer can ask which rule produced a result, then change the rule rather than argue with the output.

ResearchOps.md resolves an evolving body of ResearchOps literature into reviewed concepts, each with a clear definition and its provenance, published for people and for agents in the same source of truth. Every concept is marked as coined or redefined and carries the reasoning behind it, so the vocabulary a team argues with is inspectable rather than assumed.

Calibration Matters More Than Automation, published in The ResearchOps Review in April 2026, makes the operational argument: scaling AI in a research organization is a governance problem rather than a deployment problem. Quality, cost, and risk are research operations responsibilities, not downstream cleanup; the machine is more useful as a sparring partner than as an oracle.

The related failure is quieter than a wrong answer. An unstructured pile of documents forces an AI to read everything to find anything, billed by the word; and a model preserved perfectly can still mislead once nobody remembers which decision it was built to inform. The question has to travel with the answer.
